\begin{abstract}
\noindent Patents owned by firms in emerging markets lapse sooner than foreign-owned patents in the same office, a
gap usually read as a sign that the inventions are worth less, or that their owners lack the finance and capacity
to use them. Using renewal records for 3 million firm and university patents filed in 2000--2005 at the patent
offices of five emerging economies (China, Korea, Russia, Taiwan and the Czech Republic) and five advanced ones (the
United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France), I compare domestic and foreign owners within each
office and ask whether the domestic shortfall is larger in emerging markets. Once observable quality is held fixed,
it is not: larger emerging-market firms keep their patents longer than comparable firms in rich economies, and small
firms about as long; small Russian and, more weakly, Taiwanese firms are the exceptions. The raw shortfall reflects
the lower citation of domestic patents. Evidence from the same inventions patented at home and abroad, credit
shocks, fee changes and structural renewal models that value each patent from its own renewal history points the
same way, and holds when every eligible owner is assumed to pay discounted fees. Two differences remain: small
emerging-market firms' patents gain more from being sold, and Chinese university patents are worth well under half
as much as comparable foreign ones. Counterfactuals with the renewal model put the returns at stake in markets for
technology and in university patenting at hundreds of millions of dollars per cohort in China, against almost
nothing for fee relief.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} patent renewal; patent value; emerging markets; innovation; intellectual property;

\end{abstract}

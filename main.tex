\begin{abstract}
\noindent 
%In emerging-market patent offices, domestic firms' patents are less likely to be renewed relative to foreign-owned patents than in advanced economies

\noindent Using renewal records for 3 million firm and university patents filed in 2000--2005 at the patent
offices of five emerging economies (China, Korea, Russia, Taiwan and the Czech Republic) and five advanced ones (the
United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France), I compare domestic and foreign owners within each
office and ask whether the domestic shortfall in patent renewal is larger in emerging markets. Once observable quality is held fixed,
it is not: larger emerging-market firms keep their patents longer than comparable firms in rich economies, and small
firms about as long; small Russian and, more weakly, Taiwanese firms are the exceptions. The raw shortfall reflects
the lower citation of domestic patents. Evidence from the same inventions patented at home and abroad, credit
shocks and fee changes shows no sign that emerging-market firms are held back by finance or the cost of renewal.
Structural renewal models that value each patent from its own renewal history give the same home premium in emerging
and advanced economies for all owners together; for small firms it is smaller against a benchmark dominated by Japan
but comparable office by office. Two differences remain: small emerging-market firms' patents gain more from being
sold, and Chinese university patents are worth well under half as much as comparable foreign ones, yet survive far
longer once sold to firms. Counterfactuals with the renewal model put the returns at stake in markets for technology
at over a billion dollars per cohort in China and in university patenting at hundreds of millions, against almost
nothing for fee relief.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} patent renewal; patent value; emerging markets; innovation; intellectual property;
markets for technology\\

\end{abstract}

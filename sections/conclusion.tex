\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The apparent emerging-market gap in patent survival is mostly a matter of when the renewal clock starts and of what
gets patented. Counted from grant and at equal observable quality, firms in China, Korea and the Czech Republic keep
their patents about as long as firms in the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France, larger
firms longer, and value them at least as much. Credit shocks and fee cuts barely move their renewal decisions, and they do not drop the home patents of
inventions they still protect abroad. For firms, the binding constraint is the invention, not the inventor.

Two qualifications matter for policy. Owner capacity counts at the margin: small emerging-market firms' patents
survive much longer after a sale. Policies that deepen markets for technology, such as registries, licensing
intermediaries and secure transfer of title, may therefore add value, while short-run credit or fee relief is
unlikely to change which patents are kept. And university patenting in China is a clear weak spot: patents are
dropped sooner and are worth well under half as much as comparable foreign ones, consistent with patenting driven by
evaluation and subsidy targets rather than use. Russia, where small firms do fall behind, deserves a closer look.

Three limitations stand out. Only five emerging economies record lapses well enough to study, and two of them, Korea
and Taiwan, are now high-income; the result is weaker without them. Citations capture only part of quality. And
renewal values measure private value to the owner, not the social value of the knowledge a patent spreads.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The emerging-market shortfall in patent survival reflects what gets patented. At equal observable quality, firms in
China, Korea and the Czech Republic keep their patents about as long as firms in the United States, Japan, Germany,
the United Kingdom and France, larger firms longer, and value them about as much relative to foreign owners. Credit shocks and fee cuts barely
move their renewal decisions, and they do not drop the home patents of inventions they still protect abroad. For
firms, the binding constraint is the invention, not the inventor.

Two qualifications matter for policy, and the counterfactuals put a price on both. Owner capacity counts at the
margin: small emerging-market firms' patents survive much longer after a sale, and moving them to owners able to use
them is worth up to \$390 million a year per cohort in China. Policies that deepen markets for technology, such as
registries, licensing intermediaries and secure transfer of title, may therefore add value, while fee relief, worth
almost nothing on the same scale, is unlikely to change which patents are kept. And university patenting in China is
a clear weak spot: patents are dropped sooner and are worth well under half as much as comparable foreign ones,
consistent with patenting driven by evaluation and subsidy targets rather than use. Yet once sold to firms, the
same patents survive far longer, so part of the loss lies in who holds them: technology-transfer offices and
licensing to firms may recover much of the value. Closing the gap is worth
\$170--310 million a year per cohort. Russia, where small firms do fall behind, deserves a closer look.

Three limitations stand out. Only five emerging economies record lapses well enough to study, and two of them, Korea
and Taiwan, are now high-income; the result is weaker without them. Citations capture only part of quality. And
renewal values measure private value to the owner, not the social value of the knowledge a patent spreads.

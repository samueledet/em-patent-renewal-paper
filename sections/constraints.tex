\section{Are emerging-market owners constrained?}\label{sec:constraints}

Equal survival at equal quality does not rule out owner constraints: constrained owners might keep their patents as
long as others but get less from them, or drop patents that would be worth keeping in other hands. Three tests look
for constraints directly (Figure~\ref{fig:constraints}).\footnote{\label{fn:specs}A: inventions granted at home and in at least one other study office; patent-family and office $\times$ year effects. B: patents at risk by office, year, age and citation tercile, weighted by patents at risk; office $\times$ year, office $\times$ age $\times$ citation-tercile and office $\times$ owner-group $\times$ domestic effects; standard errors clustered by office $\times$ year. Credit-shock years (systemic banking crises in \citealp{LaevenValencia2018} and documented credit crunches): 2008--09 in the advanced economies, Taiwan and the Czech Republic; 2003--04 (credit-card crisis; \citealp{KangMa2009}) and 2008--09 in Korea; 2013 (interbank crunch;
\citealp{BIS2013}) and 2017--18 (deleveraging) in China; 2008--09 and 2014--15 (rouble crisis) in Russia. C: patents alive and unsold 1--5 years after grant, outcome at most 18 years after filing; years-since-grant $\times$ office $\times$ year, office $\times$ field and office $\times$ owner-group effects, with office-specific slopes in citations and grant lag; sales of granted patents recorded in China, Russia, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France. A and C: standard errors clustered by owner. Emerging-minus-advanced differences are interactions in one regression.} Each is shown for all firms, small firms and universities, pooled over
all offices, for the emerging and the advanced economies separately, and as the difference between the two, which is
the test of interest. Country-level results are in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}, and a fourth test based on fee changes
in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fees}.

\paragraph{Same invention, home and abroad.} Inventions patented both at home and in another study office hold the
technology fixed. With patent-family fixed effects, owners everywhere keep the home patent longer than the same
invention's foreign patents, by 7.9 points, and small firms by 13.1: the home market is where they use
the invention, and foreign protection is dropped first (panel A). If emerging-market owners found their home patents
hard to use, their home advantage would be smaller. It is larger: 11.7 points against 6.8 in the advanced
economies, a difference of 4.9, and 16.2 against 11.9 for small firms, a difference of 4.2.

\paragraph{Credit shocks.} If finance were binding, credit crunches should raise domestic owners' lapses, and small
firms' most. Following each patent year by year, with country-specific shock years (footnote~\ref{fn:specs}), domestic owners in the advanced economies lapse slightly more in shock years, by 0.9 points a year, and their small firms by 0.4 (panel B). Domestic owners in the emerging economies do not:
$-0.2$ for all firms and $-0.5$ for small firms, so the difference is negative, $-0.9$ for small
firms. Each comparison is within an office and year, so fee changes and recording practices do not drive it.

\paragraph{Sales.} One result points to owner capacity at the margin. I follow patents alive and not
yet sold 1--5 years after grant, compare those sold in the next year with those not sold, and record whether they are
alive five years later, with citations counted only up to the point of sale. By this measure, a sale raises the survival of domestic owners' patents by 5.2 points in the emerging
economies and 3.9 in the advanced ones (panel C), a difference that is not significant. For small firms the gap is
wider: 11.0 against 4.9, a difference of 6.1. Buyers pick good patents everywhere, so the premium itself does not measure what a sale
adds; the informative part is how much larger it is for small emerging-market sellers. It is largest in China
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}). Among the emerging economies, only China and Russia record sales of
granted patents: Korea records changes of owner only while the application is pending, and Taiwan and the Czech
Republic record none.

\paragraph{Universities.} Universities behave differently from firms on two of the three tests. Emerging-market
universities keep the home patent 8.1 points longer than the same invention's foreign patents, against 18.2 for advanced-economy universities, a difference of $-10.1$: unlike firms, they lean less on their home
patents. And a sale raises the survival of emerging-market university patents by 43.9 points, against $-6.3$ in
the advanced economies, almost entirely in China (45 points; Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}). Patents that Chinese universities would have dropped survive in the hands of
firms; buyers choose which patents to buy, so part of the gap is selection, but the same selection operates in the
advanced economies, where there is no premium overall. Among the advanced economies the estimate ranges from $-7$ points in
Japan to 21 in the United Kingdom, none near China's. Credit shocks do not raise universities' lapses in either group
as a whole, as expected for owners funded by public budgets rather than credit; only British and German universities
lapse more in shock years, by 6.5 and 4.4 points a year. Together with their lower survival and values at
equal quality, this suggests that for universities the problem lies partly with the owner: Chinese universities hold
patents that firms find worth keeping but that they do not use themselves.

\paragraph{Fees.} Fee changes test cash constraints at renewal time directly, but only small firms receive the
discounts, so the test is small against large firms rather than emerging against advanced. China's fee reduction
for small owners (70\% in the first three fee-years after grant, raised to 85\% and extended to six fee-years in 2016
and to ten in 2018) barely changes small firms' lapses: a 10\% lower fee lowers their lapse rate, 3.6\% a year, by
about 0.2\% of its level, not significantly, measured over all eligible firms because take-up is not observed. Small
US firms respond to changes in the three US maintenance fees about ten times as strongly (2.6\% for a 10\% fee
change); the estimate for large US firms is imprecise (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fees}). Small emerging-market firms are not more fee-sensitive
than small US firms.

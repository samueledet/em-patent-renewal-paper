\section{Are emerging-market owners constrained?}\label{sec:constraints}

Equal survival at equal quality does not rule out owner constraints: constrained owners might keep their patents as
long as others but get less from them, or drop patents that would be worth keeping in other hands. Three tests look
for constraints directly (Figure~\ref{fig:constraints}). Each is shown for all firms, small firms and universities, pooled over
all offices, for the emerging and the advanced economies separately, and as the difference between the two, which is
the test of interest. Country-level results and a fourth test based on fee changes are in Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}.

\paragraph{Same invention, home and abroad.} Inventions patented both at home and in another study office hold the
technology fixed. With patent-family fixed effects, owners everywhere keep the home patent longer than the same
invention's foreign patents, by 8.0 (0.6) points, and small firms by 13.1 (0.7): the home market is where they use
the invention, and foreign protection is dropped first (panel A). If emerging-market owners found their home patents
hard to use, their home advantage would be smaller. It is larger: 11.7 (1.3) points against 6.8 (0.7) in the advanced
economies, a difference of 4.9 (1.6), and 16.2 (1.4) against 11.9 (0.8) for small firms, a difference of 4.2 (1.7).

\paragraph{Credit shocks.} If finance were binding, credit crunches should raise domestic owners' lapses, and small
firms' most. In a patent-year panel with country-specific shock years (banking crises and documented credit crunches;
\citealp{LaevenValencia2018}), domestic owners in the advanced economies lapse slightly more in shock years, by 0.9
(0.3) points a year, and their small firms by 0.4 (0.2) (panel B). Domestic owners in the emerging economies do not:
$-0.2$ (0.7) for all firms and $-0.5$ (0.3) for small firms, so the difference is negative, $-0.9$ (0.4) for small
firms. Office $\times$ year effects absorb fee changes and recording practices.

\paragraph{Sales.} One result points to owner capacity at the margin. In a landmark design (patents alive and not
yet sold at ages 4--8, sold in the next year or not, outcome alive five years later, with citations counted only up
to the landmark age), a sale raises the survival of domestic owners' patents by 6.4 (1.3) points in the emerging
economies and 2.7 (1.1) in the advanced ones (panel C). For small firms the gap is wider: 11.8 (0.7) against 4.2
(0.8), a difference of 7.6 (1.1). Buyers select good patents everywhere, so the level of the premium is not causal;
the extra premium for small emerging-market sellers is the informative part. It is largest in Korea and China
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}). Taiwan and the Czech Republic record no ownership transfers.

\paragraph{Universities.} Universities behave differently from firms on two of the three tests. Emerging-market
universities keep the home patent 8.1 (3.3) points longer than the same invention's foreign patents, against 18.2
(2.2) for advanced-economy universities, a difference of $-10.1$ (3.9): unlike firms, they lean less on their home
patents. And a sale raises the survival of emerging-market university patents by 43.4 (2.1) points, against nothing,
$-0.2$ (3.7), in the advanced economies, almost entirely in China (45 points; Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}). Patents that Chinese universities would have dropped survive in the hands of
firms; buyers choose which patents to buy, so part of the gap is selection, but the same selection operates in the
advanced economies, where the premium is zero. Credit shocks do not raise universities' lapses in either group, as
expected for owners funded by public budgets rather than credit. Together with their lower survival and values at
equal quality, this suggests that for universities the problem lies partly with the owner: Chinese universities hold
patents that firms find worth keeping but that they do not use themselves.

\paragraph{Fees.} Fee changes test cash constraints at renewal time directly, but only small firms receive the
discounts, so the test is small against large firms rather than emerging against advanced. China's 85\% fee reduction
for small owners, whose window was extended from three to six fee-years in 2016 and to ten in 2018, lowers small
firms' lapse rate only slightly: an implied elasticity of 0.05 (intent to treat, since take-up is not observed). Small
US firms respond to stage-specific maintenance-fee changes with an elasticity of about 0.26, and large US firms not at
all (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:constraints-country}, panel D). Small emerging-market firms are not more fee-sensitive
than small US firms.

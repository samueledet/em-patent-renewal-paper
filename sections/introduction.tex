\section{Introduction}

Emerging economies now file a large share of the world's patents, but their patents are widely thought to be
worth less. One common indicator is renewal: owners pay a fee every year to keep a patent in force and stop
paying when it is no longer worth the fee, so the age at which patents lapse reveals their private value
\citep{PakesSchankerman1984,Pakes1986,SchankermanPakes1986}. By this measure the gap looks large. In the Chinese
patent office, domestic firms' patents filed in 2000--2005 were 13 percentage points less likely than
foreign owners' patents to be alive eight years after grant.

Two very different stories fit such a gap. In the first, the \emph{invention} is the problem: emerging-market
patents protect weaker technology, and owners rationally drop them sooner. In the second, the \emph{inventor} is
the problem: owners lack the finance, production capacity or market access to turn valuable inventions into
returns, so good patents lapse for want of an owner able to use them. The policy implications differ. The first
calls for investment in research capability and links to frontier technology; the second for finance, fee relief
or markets for technology.

This paper uses legal-event records from PATSTAT to separate the two for firms and universities. It makes three
points.

First, most of the raw emerging-market gap is a measurement artefact. Survival is usually counted from the filing
date, but renewal fees start at grant, and the Chinese and Korean offices grant domestic patents about two years
earlier than foreign ones, many of which enter through the international (PCT) route. Counted from filing,
domestic owners have faced two more renewal decisions by any given age. Counting from grant removes most of the
gap, while adding citation-based quality controls barely changes it (Section~\ref{sec:timing}). The point applies
to any comparison of patent survival across offices or owner types with different grant speeds.

Second, once survival is counted from grant and quality is held fixed, emerging-market firms of every size keep
their patents at least as long as comparable firms in the United States, Japan, Germany and the United Kingdom
(Section~\ref{sec:survival}). Four further pieces of evidence point the same way. Small firms keep their home
patent relatively longer than the same invention's foreign patents in every country, emerging or not. Credit
shocks do not raise small domestic firms' lapses. Small firms respond only weakly to large fee cuts. And two
structural renewal models, the second estimating the rate at which returns decline and valuing each patent from
its own renewal history without reference to its owner, give domestic-to-foreign value ratios for Chinese and
Korean firms inside the range of the rich-economy offices (Section~\ref{sec:values}).

Third, two differences remain. Patents sold by small emerging-market firms survive much longer afterwards than
patents sold by other owners, which suggests that owner capacity matters at the margin. And Chinese university
patents are the one robust weak spot: they lapse sooner and are worth well under half as much as foreign
universities' patents at equal quality.

The paper relates to work on the value of patent rights from renewal decisions
\citep{PakesSchankerman1984,Pakes1986,SchankermanPakes1986,Lanjouw1998,Bessen2008}, on indicators of patent value
\citep{HarhoffSchererVopel2003,HallJaffeTrajtenberg2005,KoganEtAl2017}, on markets for patents
\citep{Serrano2010,GalassoSchankermanSerrano2013}, and on the quality of Chinese patenting

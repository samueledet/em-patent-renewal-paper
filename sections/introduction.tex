\section{Introduction}

Emerging economies now file a large share of the world's patents, but their patents are widely thought to be worth
less. Renewal decisions offer a direct test. Owners pay a fee every year to keep a patent in force and stop paying
once it is no longer worth the fee, so the age at which patents lapse reveals what they are worth to their owners
\citep{PakesSchankerman1984,Pakes1986,SchankermanPakes1986}. By this measure the gap looks large. In the Chinese
patent office, patents filed in 2000--2005 by domestic firms were 13 percentage points less likely than those of
foreign owners to be alive eight years after grant.

Two very different stories fit such a gap. In the first, the \emph{invention} is the problem: emerging-market
patents protect weaker technology, and owners rationally drop them sooner. In the second, the \emph{inventor} is
the problem: owners lack the finance, production capacity or market access to turn valuable inventions into
returns, so good patents lapse for want of an owner able to use them. The policy answers differ. The first calls
for investment in research capability and links to frontier technology; the second for credit, fee relief or
markets in which patents can find better owners.

This paper separates the two using the legal-event records in PATSTAT, which track renewals and lapses at the
offices that report them. I first establish which offices do: of the large emerging economies, only China, Korea,
Russia, Taiwan and the Czech Republic record lapses consistently and have enough domestic firm
patents.\footnote{India, Mexico, South Africa and most of Latin America, Africa and South-East Asia report no lapses
to PATSTAT; Brazil and Hungary report them but have too few domestic firm patents (Appendix~\ref{app:data}).} I
compare them with five advanced economies (the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France), for
3 million granted patents owned by firms and universities. The design compares domestic and foreign owners in the
same office, which holds fees, law and courts fixed, and asks whether the domestic shortfall is larger in
emerging-market offices. The paper makes three points.

First, most of the raw gap is a measurement artefact. Survival is usually counted from the filing date, but renewal
fees start at grant, and emerging-market offices grant domestic patents much sooner than foreign ones: the median
small domestic firm waits 1.7 fewer years than a small foreign firm in China, 2.9 in Russia and 3.6 in
Korea.\footnote{Many foreign applications enter through the international (PCT) route, which adds up to 30 months
before national examination begins. In the advanced-economy offices the domestic--foreign difference is a year or
less.} Counted from filing, domestic owners have faced several more renewal decisions by any given age. Counting
from grant removes the gap, while adding quality controls barely changes it (Section~\ref{sec:timing}). The point
applies to any comparison of patent survival across offices, entry routes or owner types that are granted at
different speeds.

Second, once survival is counted from grant and quality is held fixed, emerging-market firms of every size keep
their patents at least as long as comparable firms in the advanced economies (Section~\ref{sec:survival}). The
result holds in China and Korea, is imprecise in the Czech Republic, and fails only for small Russian and, more
weakly, Taiwanese firms. Four further tests point the same way. Small firms keep their home patent relatively longer
than the same invention's foreign patents. Credit crunches do not raise small domestic firms' lapses. Small firms
respond only weakly to large fee cuts. And structural renewal models, one of which values each patent from its own
renewal history without reference to its owner, put the home premium of Chinese and Korean firms inside the range
of the advanced-economy offices, even if every eligible owner paid discounted fees (Section~\ref{sec:values}).

Third, two differences remain. Patents sold by small emerging-market firms survive much longer afterwards than
patents sold by other owners, which suggests that owner capacity matters at the margin and that markets for
technology move patents to better owners. And university patents in China are the one robust weak spot: they lapse
sooner and are worth well under half as much as foreign universities' patents at equal quality.

The paper contributes to three literatures. The first estimates the value of patent rights from renewal decisions
\citep{PakesSchankerman1984,Pakes1986,SchankermanPakes1986,Lanjouw1998,Bessen2008}; I extend it to emerging-market
offices and show that renewal comparisons across owners must start the clock at grant. The second studies
indicators of patent value \citep{HarhoffSchererVopel2003,HallJaffeTrajtenberg2005,KoganEtAl2017}; I value each
patent from its own renewal path rather than from its owner's characteristics. The third asks whether Chinese and
other emerging-market patenting is of low quality \citep{HuJefferson2009,LiegsalzWagner2013,DangMotohashi2015}; the
answer here is that, for firms, lower survival reflects the technology and the timing of grants, not the owners'
ability to use their patents. The sale premium connects to work on markets for patents
\citep{Serrano2010,GalassoSchankermanSerrano2013}.

Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data. Section~\ref{sec:timing} documents the timing artefact,
Section~\ref{sec:survival} compares survival at equal quality and tests for owner constraints, and
Section~\ref{sec:values} turns renewal decisions into money. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

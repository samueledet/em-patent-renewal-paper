\section{What is at stake? Three policy levers}\label{sec:stake}

The introduction set out two families of policy: if the invention is the problem, invest in research capability; if
the inventor is, relieve owners' constraints through credit, fee relief or markets for technology. The renewal model
can price the levers that the evidence points to on a common scale. I simulate each patent on its own fee path to
expiry with the deterministic model of Section~\ref{sec:values}, change one thing at a time, and report the change
in the returns owners earn from their patents per filing-year cohort, in 2024 PPP dollars and as a share of national
R\&D spending (Table~\ref{tab:levers}). Fee changes are transfers between owners and the state, so they are shown
separately and left out of the headline.

\paragraph{Fee relief.} Suppose every eligible owner took up the Chinese and Korean fee discounts. Owners would pay
less, by \$2.8 million a year for a cohort of small Chinese firms' patents, but they would keep their patents only
marginally longer, and the returns they earn would rise by less than half a million dollars. Fees are small relative
to what patents are worth, so cutting them changes few renewal decisions.

\paragraph{Markets for technology.} Suppose small domestic firms' patents earned, from filing age four, what they would
earn in the hands of a buyer. I calibrate the buyer's advantage to the extra survival of patents sold by small
emerging-market firms in Section~\ref{sec:constraints}: 7.5 points over five years, net of the premium for small
advanced-economy sellers. This requires returns about 50\% higher in China and 35\% higher in Korea. The returns from a
cohort of small firms' patents would rise by about \$310 million in China, 41\% of their value, and \$50 million in
Korea. This is an upper bound, since it reallocates every small firm's patent.

\paragraph{University patenting.} Suppose Chinese universities' patents earned what foreign universities' patents earn
in China at equal citations. A cohort would be worth about \$310 million more a year; with the typical university
home premium of the advanced-economy offices as the benchmark instead, about \$180 million. The value of Chinese
university patents would roughly double.

\paragraph{Reading the numbers.} Three cautions apply. The figures are private returns to owners, not welfare: they
leave out knowledge spillovers and the gain to users when a patent enters the public domain. The deterministic model
gives conservative levels, several times below those of the learning model. And the markets-for-technology figure is
an upper bound. The gap between the levers survives all three: relieving owners of fees is worth very little, while
helping patents reach owners who can use them and improving what Chinese universities patent are each worth hundreds
of millions of dollars per cohort, two to three orders of magnitude more.

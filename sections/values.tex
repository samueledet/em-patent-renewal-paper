\section{Patent values}\label{sec:values}

Survival is a coarse measure: it ignores how much fees differ between offices and over a patent's life.
Renewal models turn each patent's fee path and lapse decision into money.

\paragraph{Deterministic model.} Following \citet{SchankermanPakes1986}, a patent earns $r_0 d^t$ in year $t$
after grant, with $\ln r_0 \sim N(\alpha_g + \gamma \ln(1+\text{citations}), \sigma)$ by owner group $g$
(domestic or foreign $\times$ firm size or university). With returns falling and fees rising, the owner renews at
decision $j$ if returns over the period the fee covers exceed the fee, and each patent faces its own fee path. A
1\% chance per decision that the patent is dropped for reasons unrelated to value (exit, merger, strategy) keeps the
likelihood well defined; the discount factor is 0.9. The model is estimated by maximum likelihood office by office,
on up to 60{,}000 patents per group.

\paragraph{Learning and the rate of decline.} In the deterministic model the rate at which returns decline, $1-d$,
is not identified: the likelihood cannot separate fast decline with a wide spread of initial values from slow
decline with a narrow one. Following \citet{Pakes1986}, I let log returns follow a random walk with drift $\ln d$
whose shocks shrink as owners learn ($\text{sd} = s\varphi^t$), so that owners may keep a patent worth less than
its fee today for the option of later renewals. Renewal thresholds come from backward induction to expiry on each
fee path, and the model is estimated by simulated maximum likelihood. Where fees rise steeply every year, the decay
rate is identified: 0.851 in Germany and 0.840 in the UK, close to the 0.85 commonly assumed. Where fees are low
(China) or few (three US maintenance fees), it is not. I therefore fix $d = 0.85$ in all offices.

\paragraph{Results.} Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:values} reports the ratio of domestic to foreign owners' median
values at grant, at equal citations. Domestic owners value their patents more than foreign owners in the same
office, as they should, since the home market is where they sell. The question is whether that home premium is
smaller in emerging markets. It is not: for small firms the ratio is 1.9 in China and 2.0 in Korea, against
1.1--3.9 in the rich-economy offices. The learning model gives similar ratios. Chinese universities stand out at
0.4.

Panel~B reports patent-level values. Each patent is valued from its own renewal history: the learning model is
re-estimated with a single prior for all owners (only citations enter), and each patent's value is the posterior
median over simulated return paths, weighting each path by the probability of the patent's observed lapse or
survival. Owner type therefore plays no role in a patent's value. Regressing log value on domestic owner $\times$
size, with fee-path $\times$ citation-band and office $\times$ field effects, small domestic firms' patents are worth
about 20\% more than small foreign firms' in China and 30\% more in Korea, as in the US and Germany. Chinese
universities' patents are worth 58\% less than foreign universities'.

\paragraph{Levels.} A typical small domestic firm's patent is worth roughly \$6{,}000--60{,}000 at grant in the
deterministic model, and several times more in the learning model, in which early lapses reflect learning rather
than low initial value. Levels are therefore rough, and values in different offices cover protection in different
markets. The ratios, which are what the argument rests on, are similar across models and decay rates: for small
Chinese firms the ratio of raw medians is 1.20--1.25 across $d \in \{0.80, 0.85, 0.90\}$, and the ratio at equal
citations is 1.9 in the deterministic model and 1.7 in the learning model. Because a
patent's value within a fee path rises with how long it is kept, Panel~B restates the survival results in money
rather than adding an independent test.

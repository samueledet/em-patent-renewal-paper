\section{What the patents are worth}\label{sec:values}

Survival is a coarse measure: it ignores how much fees differ between offices and over a patent's life. Renewal
models turn each patent's fee path and lapse decision into money. Fees come from official schedules for each of the
ten offices by year of life and period, deflated to 2024 prices with each country's consumer price index and
converted at 2024 purchasing-power parities \citep{WDI}.

\paragraph{Two models.} In the deterministic model \citep{SchankermanPakes1986}, a patent earns $r_0 d^t$ in year $t$
after grant, with $\ln r_0 \sim N(\alpha_g + \gamma \ln(1+c), \sigma)$ for owner group $g$ (domestic or foreign
$\times$ firm size or university). With returns falling and fees rising, the owner renews while returns over the
period a fee covers exceed the fee. In this model the rate of decline $1-d$ is not identified, so I also estimate the
learning model of \citet{Pakes1986}, in which log returns follow a random walk whose shocks shrink as owners learn,
and owners may keep a patent worth less than its fee for the option of later renewals. Where fees rise steeply every year the decay rate is identified: 0.851 in Germany, 0.840 in the United Kingdom
and 0.834 in France, close to the 0.85 commonly assumed. Taiwan, whose fees rise in three-year steps, gives a slower
decline (0.93); elsewhere the rate is not identified. I fix $d = 0.85$ everywhere. Appendix~\ref{app:models} gives the details.

\paragraph{Patent-level values.} Group averages could hide the patents that matter, and they depend on how owners are
grouped. I therefore value each patent from its own renewal history: the learning model is re-estimated with a single
prior for all owners, in which only citations enter, and each patent's value is the posterior median over simulated
return paths, weighting each path by the probability of the patent's observed lapse or survival. Owner type plays no
role in a patent's value. Figure~\ref{fig:valuespooled} compares, for each owner group, the values of domestic and
foreign owners' patents on the same fee path, in the same citation band and in the same field of the same office.
This is the home premium.

\paragraph{The home premium.} Domestic owners value their patents more than foreign owners in the same office, as
they should: the home market is where they sell. Across all owners the premium is the same in the two groups of
offices, 43 log points in the emerging-market offices and 42 in the advanced ones. Mid-sized and large firms
show no difference either. Small firms do: their home premium is 17 log points in the emerging-market offices and
32 in the advanced ones, a difference of $-15$. As in Section~\ref{sec:survival}, the benchmark matters. Small
Japanese firms value their home patents far above foreign owners do in Japan (122 log points), and Japan contributes a
large share of the advanced-economy patents. Office by office, the premium of small firms is 20 log points in China and
49 in Korea, against 23 in the United States, 20 in Germany and 8 in the United Kingdom; it is close to zero in Taiwan,
the Czech Republic and France (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:patentvalues}). Universities are the exception among
emerging-market owners: Chinese universities' patents are worth 56\% less than foreign universities' in China
($1-e^{-0.83}$). The deterministic model gives the same pattern in ratios of median values (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:values}).

\paragraph{Russia.} The Russian estimates are not identified. Domestic owners paid very low fees, about \$50--150 a
year in PPP terms, but let many patents lapse, while foreign owners paid four and a half times the resident fee until
2012 and kept theirs. Read literally, the model values foreign-owned Russian patents at eight times domestic ones, with
an implausibly wide spread of initial values in the learning model. Russian patents that lapse despite near-zero fees
point to low value or to lapses unrelated to the fee, and the model cannot separate the two.

\paragraph{Fee discounts do not change the comparison.} China and Korea cut renewal fees for domestic small firms and
universities, and the models above assume standard fees. Lower fees mean the same renewal behaviour implies lower
values, so I re-estimate both models assuming that every eligible owner took the discount: in China, 30\% of the fee
in the first three fee-years after grant until 2016 and 15\% for up to ten years from 2018; in Korea, 70\% of the fee
in years 4--6 from 2014 and half from 2018. Values fall by a few percent (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:values}): the ratio
of small domestic to small foreign firms' median value is 1.88 in China and 2.41 in Korea in the deterministic model,
and 1.66 and 1.97 in the learning model. The discounts apply when fees are low, about \$250--350 a year in China,
against values in the tens of thousands.

\paragraph{Levels are rough; comparisons are not.} A typical small domestic firm's patent is worth roughly
\$5{,}000--65{,}000 at grant in the deterministic model and several times more in the learning model, in which early
lapses reflect learning rather than low initial value. Values in different offices also cover protection in
different markets. The comparisons between owners, on which the argument rests, are stable across models, decay rates
and fee assumptions, except in Russia. Because a patent's value on a given fee path rises with how long it is kept,
the patent-level values restate the survival results in money rather than adding an independent test.
